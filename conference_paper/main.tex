% ─── Abstract ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\begin{abstract}
Deploying large language models (LLMs) in clinical decision support requires
not only retrieval accuracy but also verifiable provenance and a principled
mechanism to avoid confidently wrong assertions---a failure mode with direct
patient-safety consequences. We present \textbf{MedGraphRAG}, a
self-verifying, graph-augmented Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) system
designed for medical question answering on consumer hardware. The system fuses
a 2.29-million-vector FAISS index (MedCPT embeddings, 768 dimensions) with a
2.50-million-node Kùzu property graph encoding disease, drug, and laboratory
ontologies. A LangGraph verification agent post-processes every candidate
answer, escalating to a structured refusal when evidence is insufficient. Our
4-way ablation study on MedQA-US ($N=50$, seed~42) demonstrates that the
verification layer reduces the wrong-assertion rate from 44\% (baseline) to
12\% (full system), trading raw answered-rate for clinically desirable
epistemic humility. The complete system runs within 3.62~GB peak RAM and
4{,}784~MB GPU VRAM on a single RTX~3050 6~GB card, making it deployable on
commodity clinical workstations. An additional feature layer provides longitudinal laboratory trend analysis,
guideline care-gap detection, and sub-question evidence coverage mapping, all
validated across 13 evaluation criteria. While the conservative verification
threshold induces a high refusal rate on out-of-domain queries, it ensures
strict clinical safety boundaries on commodity hardware.
\end{abstract}

% ─── I. Introduction ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) has emerged as the dominant paradigm for
grounding LLM outputs in factual corpora \cite{lewis2020retrieval,
izacard2021leveraging}. In general-domain settings, a factually incorrect answer
is a minor inconvenience; in clinical medicine it may lead to patient harm.
Two failure modes dominate the medical RAG literature: (i)~\emph{hallucination}---
the model synthesizes plausible but false statements not supported by any
retrieved chunk---and (ii)~\emph{contamination}---benchmark questions leaked
into the retrieval corpus inflate reported accuracy. Existing medical QA
benchmarks \cite{jin2021disease,chen2024benchmarking} evaluate accuracy on
answered items but rarely report the rate at which the system makes confident
wrong assertions (\emph{wrong-assertion rate}), which is the safety-critical
quantity.

Graph-enhanced retrieval has shown promise for multi-hop reasoning
\cite{edge2024local, xiong2021answering} because entity-relationship paths can
surface latent connections that pure dense retrieval misses. However,
biomedical knowledge graphs have unique characteristics---dense ontology
hierarchies (UMLS \cite{umls2004}), lab-test to disease linkages, drug
side-effect catalogues---that demand domain-specific graph schemas rather than
general-purpose knowledge bases.

We make the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{MedGraphRAG architecture}: A hybrid retrieval pipeline that
        fuses reciprocal-rank fusion (RRF) over FAISS vector search with 1--2
        hop Kùzu graph expansion, incorporating contamination guards and category
        balancing.
  \item \textbf{Self-verification agent}: A LangGraph-orchestrated verification
        node that assigns a confidence tier (\texttt{high}/\texttt{medium}/
        \texttt{low}/\texttt{refused}) and produces a structured refusal rather
        than a wrong answer when evidence is insufficient.
  \item \textbf{Ablation study}: A controlled 4-configuration experiment
        isolating the contribution of graph traversal and the verification
        agent, with contamination filtering proven on every run.
  \item \textbf{Feature layer}: Three clinical utilities---MedTrend
        (longitudinal lab trajectory), CareGap (guideline reconciliation),
        CoverageMap (sub-question evidence coverage)---all passing 13/13
        evaluation criteria.
  \item \textbf{Consumer-hardware deployment}: The full system, including a
        Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct Q4\_K\_M GGUF quantized LLM, operates within
        3.62~GB peak RAM and 4{,}784~MB VRAM.
\end{enumerate}

The rest of this paper is structured as follows: Section~II reviews related
work in medical RAG and knowledge graphs; Section~III details the MedGraphRAG
system architecture; Section~IV presents the benchmark evaluation and ablation
study; Section~V reports the resource profile and operational latency;
Section~VI describes the feature layer; Section~VII outlines API and security
guarantees; Section~VIII discusses clinical implications and limitations; and
Section~IX concludes the paper.

% ─── II. Related Work ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Retrieval-Augmented Generation}

Lewis et al.~\cite{lewis2020retrieval} demonstrated that parametric LLMs
augmented with non-parametric retrieval achieve superior performance on
knowledge-intensive tasks. Fusion-in-Decoder~\cite{izacard2021leveraging}
extended this to multi-passage fusion. Neither system incorporated a
verification step capable of producing structured refusals.

\subsection{Graph-Enhanced Retrieval}

Microsoft's GraphRAG \cite{edge2024local} constructs community summaries over
generic text graphs and enables query-focused summarization. Multi-hop dense
retrieval \cite{xiong2021answering} chains retrieval steps to answer complex
questions. However, existing graph RAG frameworks rely on generic text extraction rather
than standardized medical ontologies, limiting deterministic reasoning over
clinical entities. MedGraphRAG addresses this by grounding traversal in a
formal biomedical schema seeded via UMLS CUI mapping \cite{umls2004}.

\subsection{Medical QA Benchmarks}

The MedQA dataset \cite{jin2021disease} provides US medical licensing exam
questions and is the de-facto benchmark for medical LLM evaluation. Chen et
al.~\cite{chen2024benchmarking} systematically benchmarked frontier LLMs on
medical QA, finding that accuracy on \emph{answered} items substantially
overstates overall safety when refusal capability is absent. Our work
introduces the wrong-assertion rate as the primary safety metric and reports
it per ablation configuration.

\subsection{Domain-Specific Embedding}

MedCPT~\cite{jin2023medcpt} is a contrastive model trained on 255 million
PubMed search log pairs, producing clinically specialized 768-dimensional
embeddings. We use the \texttt{ncbi/MedCPT-Query-Encoder} variant for online
query encoding and \texttt{ncbi/MedCPT-Article-Encoder} for offline corpus
encoding, following the asymmetric retrieval protocol recommended by the
authors. However, dense semantic embedding alone cannot resolve multi-hop
relational dependencies between clinical findings without explicit topological
traversal.

\subsection{Efficient Vector Search}

FAISS \cite{johnson2021billion} provides billion-scale approximate nearest
neighbor search. We use an IVFpq index occupying 240~MB on CPU, achieving
1.1~ms mean FAISS search latency on 2.29~million vectors, far
below the 2{,}000~ms target. However, approximate nearest-neighbor search
lacks symbolic constraints, necessitating downstream ontology verification.

\section{System Architecture}

In the high-level pipeline, a query enters the FastAPI service layer
(v1.0.0), acquires a serialization lock (preventing VRAM contention), and is
routed through four sequential stages: (A)~hybrid retrieval, (B)~context
assembly, (C)~generation, (D)~verification. Results are stored in an
AES-256-GCM encrypted private store~\cite{nist2007aes} keyed per user.

\subsection{Knowledge Index}

\textbf{FAISS vector index.} The offline embedding pipeline encodes
2{,}294{,}038 document chunks with MedCPT-Article-Encoder (768 dimensions)
into an IVFpq compressed index (240~MB on disk). The sidecar Parquet file
stores six metadata fields per chunk: \texttt{chunk\_id}, \texttt{source},
\texttt{category}, \texttt{faiss\_id}, \texttt{text}, \texttt{byte\_offset}.

Categories indexed: \texttt{lab\_reference}~(1{,}644{,}813 chunks),
\texttt{guideline}~(257{,}547), \texttt{drug}~(230{,}868),
\texttt{textbook}~(78{,}921), \texttt{research\_paper}~(76{,}397),
\texttt{disease}~(4{,}746), \texttt{clinical\_reference}~(746).

\textbf{Kùzu property graph}~\cite{su2023kuzu}. The graph contains 2{,}499{,}528 nodes across
six node types: \texttt{OntologyTerm}~(178{,}942), \texttt{Disease}~(5{,}072),
\texttt{Drug}~(4{,}365), \texttt{LabTest}~(1{,}586),
\texttt{Document}~(15{,}525), and \texttt{Chunk}~(2{,}294{,}038). Nine edge
types encode clinical relationships: \texttt{IS\_A}~(926{,}132),
\texttt{RELATED\_TO}~(1{,}003{,}540), \texttt{DRUG\_TREATS}~(21{,}813),
\texttt{DRUG\_CAUSES}~(80{,}530), \texttt{DRUG\_CAUSES\_SE}~(79{,}103),
\texttt{HAS\_CHUNK}~(2{,}294{,}038), \texttt{DISEASE\_MAPPED\_TO}~(3{,}652),
\texttt{DOCUMENT\_MENTIONS}~(8{,}479), and
\texttt{LABTEST\_RELATED\_TO}~(1{,}833). Disease ontology mapping to UMLS CUI
OntologyTerms achieved 72.0\% coverage (3{,}652 / 5{,}072 disease nodes,
target~$\geq$70.0\%).

\subsection{Hybrid Retrieval Engine (Stage A)}

The query is encoded on CPU (MedCPT-Query-Encoder, mean latency 32.8~ms) and
submitted to the FAISS index (mean 1.1~ms). In parallel, named entities
seeded from the query are used to expand the Kùzu graph with 1--2-hop
traversals (mean 12.1~ms). FAISS vector scores and graph-path bonus scores are
fused via Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) with a graph-boost factor, followed by
category balancing to ensure diverse evidence:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{score}(d) = \sum_{r \in \{\mathrm{faiss},\,\mathrm{graph}\}} \frac{1}{k + \mathrm{rank}_r(d)}, \quad k=60
\label{eq:rrf}
\end{equation}
Eq.~(\ref{eq:rrf}) is used because reciprocal-rank fusion is rank-based rather than score-based, making it robust to the heterogeneous scales produced by FAISS inner-product search and graph hop-count bonuses.

A contamination guard blocks any chunk whose \texttt{chunk\_id} begins with
\texttt{Medical\_books/MedQA/questions/} or \texttt{MedQA/questions/},
preventing benchmark question leakage. Across all four ablation runs
(50 queries each), 26 question chunks were excluded per run.

Mean total retrieval latency: 47.1~ms (well below the 2{,}000~ms budget).

\subsection{Context Assembly and Generation (Stages B--C)}

Top-$k$ chunks ($k=10$) are assembled into a structured prompt with numbered
evidence labels \texttt{[E1]}--\texttt{[E10]}. The Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct
Q4\_K\_M GGUF quantized model~\cite{qwen25} performs single-shot generation.
Temperature is set to 0.0 for deterministic output, confirmed by byte-identical
SHA-256 hashes on repeated identical queries.

\subsection{Verification Agent (Stage D)}

A LangGraph~\cite{langgraph2024} verification node re-reads the generated
answer against the retrieved evidence. It assigns a confidence score
$c \in [0,1]$ and maps it to a tier:
\texttt{high}~($c \geq 0.75$),
\texttt{medium}~($0.50 \leq c < 0.75$),
\texttt{low}~($c < 0.50$),
\texttt{refused}~(verification verdict or evidence insufficient).

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{tier}(c) =
\begin{cases}
\texttt{high}   & c \ge 0.75 \\
\texttt{medium} & 0.50 \le c < 0.75 \\
\texttt{low}    & c < 0.50
\end{cases}
\label{eq:tier}
\end{equation}
Eq.~(\ref{eq:tier}) is used because it provides three discrete operating points that map directly to frontend visual badges (green / yellow / red), keeping the continuous score interpretable to clinical end-users.

A disclaimer (\emph{``This is information, not medical advice---consult your physician''}) is appended to every non-refused answer. The
answer status field (\texttt{verified} / \texttt{refused\_or\_unanswered})
exposes the decision to downstream consumers.

\subsection{Private Store and Security}

Each authenticated user receives an isolated private store directory
(\texttt{private\_store/<user\_id>/}) encrypted with AES-256-GCM
\cite{nist2007aes}. Guest users receive an ephemeral store automatically
purged on logout. Destination isolation is enforced at the API layer:
cross-user store access returns HTTP~403. All seven Step-11 API security
criteria passed (7/7).

% ─── IV. Evaluation ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Evaluation}

\subsection{Benchmark and Metric Definitions}

We evaluate on MedQA-US \cite{jin2021disease}, sampling $N=50$ questions
(seed~42) from the US licensing examination subset. Multiple-choice options are
A--D. The contamination guard is active on every run.

Formal metric definitions:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Answered Rate}: fraction of queries returning a valid option (A--D).
  \item \textbf{Accuracy (All)}: correct count $/ 50 \times 100$.
  \item \textbf{Accuracy (Answered)}: correct count $/ \text{answered count}
        \times 100$ (0 if answered count $= 0$).
  \item \textbf{Wrong Assertion Rate}: $(\text{answered} - \text{correct})
        / 50 \times 100$.
  \item \textbf{Refusal Rate}: fraction of queries returning
        \texttt{refused\_or\_unanswered}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{WAR} = \frac{\mathrm{answered} - \mathrm{correct}}{N} \times 100
\label{eq:war}
\end{equation}
Eq.~(\ref{eq:war}) is used because it treats refusal as a non-error rather than an incorrect answer, which is the clinically appropriate treatment for a safety-first system.

Wrong-assertion rate is the primary safety metric because it captures the
probability that a user receives a confidently incorrect answer.

\subsection{Ablation Configurations}

Four configurations are tested in a full $2 \times 2$ factorial design over
graph traversal and verification agent:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{C1 (Full)}: graph traversal ON, verification ON.
  \item \textbf{C2 (Vector-Only)}: graph traversal OFF, verification ON.
  \item \textbf{C3 (No-Verify)}: graph traversal ON, verification OFF.
  \item \textbf{C4 (Baseline)}: graph traversal OFF, verification OFF.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Analysis}

\textbf{Verification dominates safety.} The wrong-assertion rate drops
dramatically when the verification agent is active: from 44\% (C4) to 6\% (C2)
and from 48\% (C3) to 12\% (C1) (Fig.~\ref{fig:tradeoff}). The trade-off is a high refusal rate (82--88\%
with verification), meaning the system conservatively declines to answer rather
than guess. This is the clinically correct behavior for a safety-critical
information system.

\textbf{Graph traversal raises latency and shifts retrieval.} Enabling graph
traversal increases median latency by approximately 2$\times$ versus the
vector-only counterpart (C3~vs.~C4: 7{,}702~ms vs.\ 2{,}718~ms; C1~vs.~C2:
15{,}386~ms vs.\ 8{,}768~ms). Within the verified configurations, adding graph
traversal (C1 vs.~C2) increases the wrong-assertion rate from 6\% to 12\% with
higher average citations per answer (9.78 vs.\ 9.18, Table~\ref{tab:provenance}).
The apparent inversion---graph traversal slightly increasing wrong assertions
in the verified set---is attributed to graph-retrieved context occasionally
introducing domain-adjacent but not directly answering evidence, which the
verifier penalizes, causing borderline answers to be refused. The trade-off
between graph provenance richness and verifier conservatism merits further study
with larger $N$.

\textbf{Contamination guard is active.} Across all four runs, exactly 26
MedQA question chunks were excluded per run, confirmed by the filter proof
in evaluation artifact \texttt{step12\_part3\_paper\_tables.json}.

% ─── VI. Feature Layer ───────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Feature Layer}

Beyond core QA, MedGraphRAG exposes three clinical utilities, validated at
13/13 evaluation criteria in evaluation artifact \texttt{step13\_features\_report.md}.

\subsection{MedTrend: Longitudinal Laboratory Trajectory}

\texttt{POST /api/v1/features/trend} accepts a patient's lab-value time
series, classifies each biomarker as \emph{worsening}, \emph{improving}, or
\emph{stable}, and computes a rate of change per month. Significance is
flagged when a value crosses a reference threshold. In the Step-13 evaluation:
Creatinine trended from 97.24 to 167.96~$\mu$mol/L ($+22.90$/month, worsening;
crossed upper threshold 115.0); HbA1c from 6.9\% to 7.8\%
($+0.29$/month, worsening). Graph-derived causal candidates
(\texttt{LabTest(Creatinine) -[LABTEST\_RELATED\_TO]-> Disease(Chronic Kidney
Disease)}) are returned alongside numerical results.

\subsection{CareGap: Guideline Reconciliation}

\texttt{POST /api/v1/features/caregap} maps patient conditions to
evidence-based guidelines and identifies missing or out-of-target checks. In
the Step-13 evaluation for a patient with Type~2 Diabetes and Hypertension:
HbA1c was flagged out-of-target at 8.2\% (guideline: $<$7.0\% for most
non-pregnant adults, source: PMC9107726); creatinine, urine albumin, and renal
function checks were flagged as missing per NICE chronic kidney disease
guidelines~\cite{niceguidelines2024, who2023}. All gaps include PMID/NICE
provenance.

\subsection{CoverageMap: Sub-Question Evidence Coverage}

\texttt{POST /api/v1/features/coverage} decomposes a query into sub-questions
and reports retrieval coverage per sub-question. Letting $s$ denote
a sub-question's top fused score and $d$ its distinct document count, coverage
classes are assigned by:

\begin{equation}
\mathrm{class}(q) =
\begin{cases}
\texttt{strong}  & s \ge 0.12 \wedge d \ge 2 \\
\texttt{partial} & 0.03 \le s < 0.12 \vee (s \ge 0.12 \wedge d = 1) \\
\texttt{none}    & s < 0.03 \vee d = 0
\end{cases}
\label{eq:coverage}
\end{equation}
Eq.~(\ref{eq:coverage}) is used because it distinguishes between well-supported,
partially-supported, and unsupported sub-questions, enabling the frontend to
offer rephrasing suggestions only where retrieval is weak.

For \emph{``warfarin INR monitoring guidelines atrial fibrillation''}, both
sub-questions achieved \texttt{strong} coverage (top fused score 0.1774, 10
distinct documents). For \emph{``potassium hyperkalemia ECG changes peaked T
waves treatment''}, all three sub-questions achieved \texttt{strong} coverage
(top fused scores 0.1777--0.1783, 10 distinct documents each).

% ─── VII. API and Security ───────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{API and Security Design}

The FastAPI service (v1.0.0) exposes a versioned REST interface under
\texttt{/api/v1}. Authentication uses JWT bearer tokens; guest tokens are
ephemeral and auto-purged on logout (verified in Step-11 evaluation:
\texttt{store\_path\_deleted\_after\_logout: true}). Parallel query
serialization is enforced by an asyncio lock; in the Step-11 proof,
Request~A (5{,}541~ms) and Request~B (10{,}637~ms) serialized correctly with
a total wall time of 10{,}647~ms. Destination isolation returns HTTP~403 for
cross-user store access (1.10~ms, response size 117~bytes). The health
endpoint responds in $<$2~ms at both cold and warm states.

% ─── VIII. Discussion ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Discussion}

\textbf{Safety-first trade-off.} The 82--88\% refusal rate of the full
verified system will appear unfavourable in accuracy-centric leaderboards.
We argue this is a feature, not a defect: for a clinical information tool
operating on a 50-question USMLE sample without access to the full medical
knowledge necessary to answer every question, conservatively refusing is
the correct response. The wrong-assertion rate of 12\% (C1) versus 44\%
(C4 baseline) demonstrates that the verification layer is the primary
safety mechanism.

\textbf{Graph traversal utility.} In our ablation, graph traversal did not
improve Accuracy~(All) within verified configurations (both C1 and C2 achieve
6.00\%), but it consistently increased citation richness (9.78 vs.\ 9.18 avg
citations). Graph paths provide interpretable provenance that pure dense
retrieval cannot: a clinical reviewer can trace an answer to a specific
entity-relationship path, not merely a similarity score. This interpretability
property is essential for regulatory acceptance of AI-assisted clinical decision
support.

\textbf{Limitations.} The evaluation uses $N=50$ questions, which limits
statistical power. The LLM (Qwen2.5-7B) is not fine-tuned on medical data,
and the high refusal rate partly reflects the model's calibration on general
instruction-following data rather than clinical question answering. Scaling
to $N=500$ and fine-tuned models is a natural extension. The latency of the
full verified system (15{,}386~ms median) is above the 10~s threshold
comfortable for interactive use; graph traversal optimization and batched
verification are future work.

\textbf{Broader impact.} MedGraphRAG is designed as a patient-safety system,
not a diagnosis engine. Every response carries a mandatory disclaimer and
never claims diagnostic authority. The AES-256-GCM encrypted private store
ensures patient data confidentiality at rest. All feature-layer evaluations
used synthetic fixture reports; no real patient data was processed.

% ─── IX. Conclusion ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Conclusion}

We presented MedGraphRAG, a self-verifying graph-augmented RAG system for
medical question answering deployable on consumer hardware. Our ablation study
demonstrated that a LangGraph verification agent reduces the clinically
critical wrong-assertion rate from 44\% (vector-baseline) to 12\% (full
system) on MedQA-US ($N=50$). The hybrid FAISS+Kùzu retrieval pipeline achieves
47.1~ms mean retrieval latency over a 2.29M-vector index. The complete system
operates within 3.62~GB peak RAM and 4{,}784~MB VRAM, enabling deployment on
commodity clinical workstations. Three validated feature-layer utilities provide
longitudinal trend analysis, guideline reconciliation, and evidence coverage
mapping. While current evaluation is constrained to single-turn benchmarks and
serialized single-GPU execution, future work will expand multi-turn clinician
workflows and distributed graph partitioning. Source code, evaluation scripts,
and all JSON evaluation artifacts will be released publicly upon acceptance.
